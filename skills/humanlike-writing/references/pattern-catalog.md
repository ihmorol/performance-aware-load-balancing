# Humanlike Writing Pattern Catalog

This catalog converts the 144 patterns identified in the source conversation into operational guidance. A pattern is a revision signal, not proof that a person or model wrote the text. Apply the rules to newly authored prose and editable prose supplied for revision. Preserve supplied facts and confidence, exact quotations, code, identifiers, citations, legal wording, technical terms, required templates, and formats whose accuracy depends on exact reproduction. Do not preserve an entire supplied draft verbatim when the user asks for a rewrite. Accuracy, evidence, meaning, accessibility, and explicit user instructions outrank style rules.

All examples in this catalog are fictional teaching examples. They are never evidence for a real claim. When editing real text, preserve the supplied facts and level of certainty. Add a name, number, date, cause, result, source, personal view, or limitation only when the user supplied it or an independent source verified it. If a useful concrete replacement cannot be grounded, delete the unsupported claim, mark it unverified, or ask for the missing fact. Do not copy fictional details from this catalog into real work.

Evidence labels:

- **Research association:** observed in comparative or corpus research; it does not identify authorship by itself.
- **Checker flag:** a disclosed heuristic used by at least one style or AI-writing checker; other detectors may ignore it.
- **Editorial heuristic:** a reader or editor may notice it; ordinary human writing may contain it.
- **Artifact/fact check:** inspectable residue or a verifiable reference problem; it may show tool involvement or poor editing, but not who wrote the surrounding prose.
- **Wikipedia context:** relevant to Wikipedia editing and markup; it is not a general prose rule.
- **Technical metric:** requires a specified model, classifier, or computation; it cannot be inferred reliably from a sentence or optimized as a writing target.

## Rhythm, repetition, and statistical regularity

| ID | Original pattern | Strict operational rule | Original example | Better example or exact action | Evidence and exception |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| RH-01 | Repeated sentence rhythm | Revise any run of three sentences with nearly identical length and cadence. Vary length only when the content supports the change. | “The team reviewed the proposal. The manager approved the schedule. The staff completed the project.” | “After the team reviewed the proposal, the manager approved the schedule. Staff completed the project.” | Research association reported for sentence regularity and checker flag; parallel rhythm is allowed for deliberate emphasis, instructions, or poetry. Source pointers: [desaire](sources-and-limits.md#source-desaire), [th-method](sources-and-limits.md#source-th-method). |
| RH-02 | Repeated grammatical skeleton | Do not reuse the same grammatical frame across three adjacent claims unless the repetition carries meaning. | “The policy supports growth. The platform supports learning. The program supports innovation.” | “The policy supports growth, while the platform helps people learn. The program encourages innovation.” | Editorial heuristic; keep parallel syntax in comparisons, lists, and formal requirements. Source pointers: [rhythm](sources-and-limits.md#source-rhythm), [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RH-03 | Repeated sentence openings | Avoid three adjacent sentences that begin with the same word or phrase unless deliberate anaphora is required. | “This improves access. This reduces delays. This strengthens trust.” | “This improves access and reduces delays. It also strengthens trust.” | Editorial heuristic; preserve deliberate rhetoric and exact procedural steps. Source pointers: [counter](sources-and-limits.md#source-counter). |
| RH-04 | Uniform paragraph length | Let paragraph length follow the size of the idea. Do not pad or split paragraphs merely to make them visually equal. | Every paragraph contains three sentences of roughly twenty words each. | Combine a small supporting point with its claim, and give a complex limitation enough space to stand alone. | Research association reported for paragraph-length regularity and editorial heuristic; forms, abstracts, and house styles may impose fixed lengths. Source pointers: [desaire](sources-and-limits.md#source-desaire), [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RH-05 | Uniform paragraph function | Give each paragraph the function the argument needs; do not repeat one claim-example-importance template throughout. | Claim → generic example → sentence explaining why it matters, repeated throughout. | Use one paragraph for evidence, another for a limitation, and a short final paragraph for the decision. | Editorial heuristic; repeated structures are valid in reference manuals and standardized reports. Source pointers: [rhythm](sources-and-limits.md#source-rhythm). |
| RH-06 | Uniform readability | Match sentence complexity to the material instead of forcing every section into the same reading level. | A technical explanation, anecdote, and conclusion all use equally simple sentence structures. | Explain the technical mechanism precisely, tell the anecdote plainly, and state the conclusion briefly. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; accessibility requirements may call for consistently plain language. Source pointers: [th-method](sources-and-limits.md#source-th-method). |
| RH-07 | Mechanical long-short alternation | Do not alternate sentence lengths by formula. Revise for emphasis and comprehension, not a preset pattern. | “The committee discussed every proposed change before approving the revised plan. Progress. The staff then implemented every recommendation.” | “After discussing each change, the committee approved the plan. Staff then implemented its recommendations.” | Editorial heuristic; a short sentence may be useful when it earns emphasis. Source pointers: [rhythm](sources-and-limits.md#source-rhythm). |
| RH-08 | Manufactured staccato | Use fragments sparingly and only when they clarify voice or emphasis. Never stack generic fragments as a dramatic device. | “A new beginning. A bold move. A lasting impact.” | “The decision began a new phase for the organization.” | Editorial heuristic; fragments are valid in dialogue, labels, slogans, and intentional literary prose. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| RH-09 | Repeated punctuation habits | Do not force several nearby sentences into the same punctuation template. | “The plan is simple: reduce costs. The goal is clear: improve service. The result is obvious: growth.” | “The plan reduces costs, improves service, and produces growth.” | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; repeated punctuation is fine in structured definitions or examples. Source pointers: [counter](sources-and-limits.md#source-counter), [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RH-10 | Narrow vocabulary | Replace vague repeated words with the correct supplied terms, but do not rotate synonyms merely for variety. | “The important change produced important benefits for an important project.” | “The change benefited the project.” | Editorial heuristic; repeat an exact technical term when substitution would reduce precision. Source pointers: [linguistic](sources-and-limits.md#source-linguistic), [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RH-11 | Phrase repetition | Remove accidental repetition of a distinctive phrase unless it is a defined term or necessary refrain. | “Meaningful progress” appears in the introduction, every section, and the conclusion. | Replace the repeated phrase with a supplied metric or concrete change. If neither exists, state the claim once. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; preserve key terms, quotations, branding, and deliberate refrains. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RH-12 | Excessively smooth transitions | Omit transitions that restate an obvious relationship. Use a connector only when it clarifies logic. | “The road closed. Consequently, vehicles could no longer travel along the closed road.” | “The closure stopped vehicle traffic on the road.” | Editorial heuristic; retain explicit connectors when accessibility, legal interpretation, or complex reasoning needs them. Source pointers: [rhythm](sources-and-limits.md#source-rhythm). |

## Vocabulary and stock phrasing

| ID | Original pattern | Strict operational rule | Original example | Better example or exact action | Evidence and exception |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| VX-01 | Clusters of “AI vocabulary” | Remove clusters of fashionable abstract words. Replace each with a supplied action, fact, or consequence. | “We delve into this pivotal development, underscoring its profound implications.” | Name the development and its verified effect. If neither is supplied, delete the sentence. | Research association for population-level vocabulary shifts and checker flag; a listed word may be correct in a quotation or technical context. Source pointers: [kobak](sources-and-limits.md#source-kobak), [russell](sources-and-limits.md#source-russell). |
| VX-02 | Corporate buzzword stacking | Do not stack business jargon. Name who acts, what they do, and what changes only when those details are supplied or verified. | “We leverage synergies to unlock scalable value across the ecosystem.” | If independently supplied or verified: “[Named teams] will share [specific resource], which is expected to [supported effect].” Otherwise, state only the documented action. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; preserve required corporate terminology when it has a defined meaning. Source pointers: [prowlo-vocab](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-vocab), [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| VX-03 | Promotional adjective stacking | Do not pile flattering adjectives onto a product or idea. Support one necessary descriptor with evidence. | “Our innovative, robust, seamless platform delivers transformative results.” | If independently supplied or verified: “The platform processed [measured volume] under [test conditions].” Otherwise, remove the promotional adjectives. | Editorial heuristic; marketing copy may require a house voice, but factual support remains necessary. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer), [wiki](sources-and-limits.md#source-wiki). |
| VX-04 | Ornate abstract nouns | Replace ornamental abstractions and grand metaphors with the supplied object or effect. | “The initiative enriches the tapestry of the educational landscape.” | If independently supplied or verified: “The initiative adds [specific educational service] at [verified locations].” Otherwise, identify the initiative without claiming an effect. | Editorial heuristic; preserve established figurative language in literary work or quotations. Source pointers: [prowlo-vocab](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-vocab), [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| VX-05 | Inflated verbs | Prefer the simplest accurate verb. | “The application facilitates the utilization of available resources.” | “The application makes available resources easier to use.” | Editorial heuristic; use formal verbs when they carry a distinct legal or technical meaning. Source pointers: [prowlo-vocab](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-vocab). |
| VX-06 | Empty significance words | Do not call something important, significant, or crucial without stating why and to whom. | “This significant initiative represents an important development with crucial implications.” | If independently supplied or verified: “The initiative [specific change], which affects [named group] by [supported consequence].” Otherwise, remove the significance claim. | Editorial heuristic; the terms are allowed when the following evidence defines the significance. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| VX-07 | Magic adverbs | Remove adverbs that imply depth, causality, or transformation without showing it. | “The dashboard quietly and fundamentally reshapes collaboration.” | If independently supplied or verified: “The dashboard [specific function] for [named users].” Otherwise, describe only the dashboard feature present in the source. | Editorial heuristic; retain an adverb when it reports an observable manner or measured degree. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| VX-08 | Stock importance prefaces | State the fact directly instead of announcing that the reader should notice it. | “It is important to note that registration closes Friday.” | “Registration closes Friday.” | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; a warning label may be needed for safety-critical information. Source pointers: [prowlo-vocab](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-vocab). |
| VX-09 | Generic modern-world opening | Begin with the supplied subject, event, or problem. Do not open with a broad claim about today’s world. | “In today’s rapidly evolving digital world, communication is more important than ever.” | Name the actual communication problem and affected group. If the source gives neither, delete the opening. | Editorial heuristic; broad context is allowed when the historical comparison is supported and relevant. Source pointers: [th-patterns](sources-and-limits.md#source-th-patterns). |
| VX-10 | Stock topic transitions | Replace formulaic topic introductions with a direct statement. | “When it comes to budgeting, planning plays a key role.” | “Budgeting requires planning.” | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; conversational transitions are fine when they sound natural and add orientation. Source pointers: [prowlo-vocab](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-vocab). |
| VX-11 | Connector saturation | Do not begin consecutive sentences with formal connectors. Keep only those needed to mark a real logical relationship. | “Furthermore, costs fell. Moreover, sales rose. Additionally, retention improved.” | “Costs fell, sales rose, and retention improved.” | Research association reported for connector frequency and editorial heuristic; formal argument may need explicit logical connectors. Source pointers: [th-method](sources-and-limits.md#source-th-method), [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| VX-12 | Clichéd trade-off language | Name the supplied benefit and cost instead of using a stock metaphor. | “Automation is a double-edged sword.” | Name the independently supported benefit and cost. If neither is supplied, do not assert a trade-off. | Editorial heuristic; a familiar metaphor is acceptable when it is the clearest phrasing for the audience. Source pointers: [prowlo-vocab](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-vocab). |

## Sentence constructions and rhetorical templates

| ID | Original pattern | Strict operational rule | Original example | Better example or exact action | Evidence and exception |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| RT-01 | “Not just X, but Y” | Avoid this construction unless the distinction corrects a real misunderstanding. State the supported claim directly. | “This is not just a calendar; it is a gateway to productivity.” | “This calendar supports productivity.” | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; retain when the contrast is factually necessary. Source pointers: [prowlo-check](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-check), [th-patterns](sources-and-limits.md#source-th-patterns). |
| RT-02 | “Not X, but Y” | Do not manufacture drama with corrective contrast. Use it only when rejecting a specific plausible claim. | “The problem is not technology. The problem is mindset.” | “Mindset is the problem.” | Editorial heuristic; valid in rebuttals that identify the rejected claim. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| RT-03 | Negation chains | Do not stack fragments that deny vague alternatives before revealing a positive claim. | “Not a shortcut. Not a compromise. A revolution.” | Replace the chain with its one supported positive claim. If “revolution” is unsubstantiated, delete it. | Editorial heuristic; deliberate literary or campaign rhetoric may use repetition. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| RT-04 | Rule-of-three saturation | Do not force ideas into groups of three. Include exactly the items the evidence supports. | “Build trust, drive growth, and unlock potential.” | Replace each abstraction with a supplied action or result. If none is available, remove the list. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; natural three-item sets and required lists are fine. Source pointers: [prowlo-check](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-check). |
| RT-05 | Colon followed by a triple | Avoid repeatedly introducing three abstract nouns after a colon. State the supplied requirements in natural syntax. | “Success requires three things: clarity, consistency, and commitment.” | List only the independently supplied or verified requirements, even if the result has fewer or more than three items. | Editorial heuristic; colons and three-item lists are valid when they fit the content. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RT-06 | Perfectly balanced clauses | Break repeated mirror constructions unless the symmetry clarifies a genuine comparison. | “Technology creates opportunity; leadership creates direction.” | “Technology creates opportunities that leaders direct.” | Editorial heuristic; preserve parallelism in contrasts, speeches, and memorable definitions. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| RT-07 | Rhetorical question-answer pairs | Ask a question only when the reader must consider it. Do not use a question as a decorative lead-in to your own answer. | “The result? A complete transformation.” | “The result was a complete transformation.” If that claim lacks evidence, replace it with the verified result. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; FAQ formats and genuine inquiry are exceptions. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| RT-08 | Stacked rhetorical questions | Do not stack questions for momentum. Convert them into the actual decision criteria. | “Why now? Why this team? Why this approach?” | “The proposal must explain its timing, ownership, and method.” | Editorial heuristic; interviews, surveys, and teaching prompts may require multiple questions. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RT-09 | False suspense hooks | State ordinary facts without reveal language. | “Here’s the kicker: the subscription renews monthly.” | “The subscription renews monthly.” | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; narrative writing may use suspense when a reveal truly matters. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer), [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| RT-10 | False ranges | Use “from X to Y” only for a real continuum, sequence, or bounded scope. | “From ancient philosophy to mobile applications, connection matters.” | Name the actual endpoints and their relationship. If no meaningful axis exists, remove the range. | Editorial heuristic; valid ranges should have a meaningful axis. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| RT-11 | Universal-audience framing | Name the supplied audience instead of using an all-purpose “whether you are” formula. | “Whether you’re a student or a global executive, this tool is for you.” | Identify the verified target audience. If the tool genuinely serves both groups, explain the supported use for each. | Editorial heuristic; broad audiences are allowed when evidence and product scope support them. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RT-12 | Generic concession | Specify the supplied benefit, limitation, and condition instead of offering empty balance. | “While the approach offers promise, challenges remain.” | If independently supplied or verified: “[Measured benefit], but [documented limitation].” Otherwise, do not invent either side of the concession. | Editorial heuristic; concise concessions are useful when their referents are already clear. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RT-13 | Superficial trailing analysis | Do not attach an “-ing” clause that invents significance or causality. | “The office opened in June, highlighting the company’s commitment to excellence.” | “The office opened in June.” Add effects only when independently supplied or verified. | Wikipedia context and editorial heuristic; retain participial clauses that accurately describe simultaneous action. Source pointers: [wiki](sources-and-limits.md#source-wiki). |
| RT-14 | Unnecessary parenthetical nuance | Remove asides that perform nuance without changing meaning. Put necessary qualifications in a direct clause. | “The system improves outcomes—and, perhaps more importantly, possibilities.” | “The system improves outcomes.” | Editorial heuristic; parenthetical detail is fine when it is relevant and concise. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| RT-15 | Avoiding “is” and “has” | Use “is” and “has” when they are the clearest verbs. Do not inflate simple descriptions with “serves as” or “stands as.” | “The library serves as a repository of books and stands as a hub of learning.” | “The library has books and supports learning.” | Wikipedia context and editorial heuristic; “serves as” is valid when describing a function rather than identity. Source pointers: [wiki](sources-and-limits.md#source-wiki). |
| RT-16 | Generic aphorism | Remove slot-filled maxims. Replace them with a supplied claim that can be explained or tested. | “Trust is the currency of tomorrow.” | State the verified action or condition that affects trust. If none is supplied, delete the aphorism. | Editorial heuristic; preserve attributed quotations and intentional literary prose. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |

## Document structure and information density

| ID | Original pattern | Strict operational rule | Original example | Better example or exact action | Evidence and exception |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ST-01 | Announce-then-answer | Begin with the answer or necessary context. Do not announce what the section will explain. | “This section will explain the reasons behind the decision.” | State the supplied reasons directly. If no reasons are available, do not imply that the section contains them. | Editorial heuristic; roadmaps are useful in long technical or academic documents. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| ST-02 | Prompt echo | Do not restate the request unless clarification, scope, or a short confirmation requires it. | “You are asking why the server stopped. Understanding why it stopped is important.” | State the verified cause of the server stop directly. If it is unknown, say that the cause is unknown. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; restatement is valid when resolving ambiguity. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| ST-03 | Compulsive counting | Do not announce item counts unless the count helps navigation or proves completeness. | “There are four key considerations and three essential implications.” | Present only the supplied considerations and implications. Announce their counts only when navigation or completeness requires it. | Editorial heuristic; numbered procedures and audit checklists should retain counts. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| ST-04 | List-shaped prose | Use a real list for parallel items, or connect the ideas logically. Do not disguise enumeration as repetitive prose. | “The first benefit is speed. The second benefit is flexibility. The third benefit is reliability.” | “The benefits are speed, flexibility, and reliability.” | Editorial heuristic; formal speeches and exam answers may require enumerated prose. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| ST-05 | Excessive sectioning | Add a heading only when it helps readers navigate a distinct section. | A hundred-word email contains “Background,” “Analysis,” “Implications,” and “Conclusion.” | Use plain paragraphs, adding a break only when the email changes subject or action. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; required templates control heading structure. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| ST-06 | Padded enumeration | Remove overlapping list items and keep only distinct points. | “Benefits: speed, faster work, improved efficiency, reduced delays, quicker results.” | “Benefits: faster work and fewer delays.” | Editorial heuristic; repeat terms when a regulated checklist requires exact categories. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| ST-07 | Bare marketing bullets | Every bullet must state a supplied, checkable feature, action, result, or constraint. | “Superior performance. Enhanced reliability. Exceptional flexibility.” | If independently supplied or verified: “[Measured result] under [documented conditions].” Otherwise, remove the unsupported bullet. | Editorial heuristic; slogans may be requested, but factual claims still need support. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| ST-08 | Paragraph interchangeability | Order paragraphs by dependency, chronology, cause, or argumentative purpose. If they can be shuffled unchanged, add the missing relationships. | Separate “efficiency,” “innovation,” and “collaboration” paragraphs can be shuffled unchanged. | Put the supplied cause before its effect, and place a documented limitation beside the claim it qualifies. | Research association reported for related formulaic document structure and editorial heuristic; independent glossary or reference entries may be reorderable. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer), [russell](sources-and-limits.md#source-russell). |
| ST-09 | One-point dilution | State a claim once, then add supplied evidence, qualification, or consequence. Delete paraphrases that add nothing. | “Communication matters. Dialogue is essential. Conversation drives success.” | State the communication claim once, then add verified evidence or stop. | Editorial heuristic; strategic repetition may aid accessibility or instruction. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| ST-10 | Full content duplication | Delete repeated passages unless repetition is required for self-contained reference sections. | The same explanation occurs word for word in sections two and six. | Keep one authoritative explanation and link or refer back to it. | Artifact/fact check and editorial heuristic; contracts, forms, and independent modules may require duplication. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| ST-11 | Fractal summaries | Summarize only where the document’s length or complexity warrants it. Do not recap every subsection and then recap the recaps. | “This section showed…” follows every subsection, then another overall summary appears. | Keep one synthesis after the full analysis. | Editorial heuristic; educational material may use planned retrieval summaries. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| ST-12 | Recap-only conclusion | End with the supplied decision, implication, limitation, or next action. Omit a conclusion that merely repeats headings. | “In conclusion, planning, communication, and teamwork are important for success.” | End with the actual decision or next action. If none is supplied, stop after the final substantive point. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; abstracts and formal reports may require concise recaps. Source pointers: [th-patterns](sources-and-limits.md#source-th-patterns). |
| ST-13 | Never-ending conclusion | Write one ending. Delete successive takeaway signals and repeated final lessons. | “Ultimately… In short… Above all… The real takeaway…” | State the final implication once, then stop. | Editorial heuristic; multiple closing sections may be required by a template. Source pointers: [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| ST-14 | Low information density | Replace vague abstractions with supplied facts, mechanisms, examples, or decisions. | “The initiative demonstrates the importance of meaningful efforts toward positive outcomes.” | If independently supplied or verified: “The initiative [specific action] for [named group] with [measured result].” Otherwise, remove the unsupported outcome claim. | Editorial heuristic; concise context or tone-setting language may sometimes be useful. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |

## Tone, certainty, and authorial voice

| ID | Original pattern | Strict operational rule | Original example | Better example or exact action | Evidence and exception |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| TN-01 | Hedge-free absolute claims | Do not use absolute certainty unless the evidence proves an invariant. State scope, conditions, and uncertainty. | “This method eliminates bias and guarantees accurate results.” | If independently supplied or verified: “In [defined scope], the method [measured effect]; it may not apply beyond [known limit].” Otherwise, remove the guarantee. | Research association reported for certainty-related linguistic features and checker flag; exact mathematical, contractual, or tested invariants may be absolute. Source pointers: [desaire](sources-and-limits.md#source-desaire), [th-method](sources-and-limits.md#source-th-method). |
| TN-02 | Gratuitous universal scope | Use “all,” “every,” and “never” only when the claim’s domain is explicit and verified. | “Every successful organization follows this approach.” | If independently supplied or verified: “[Defined organizations] in [named study] used this approach.” Otherwise, remove the universal claim. | Editorial heuristic; universal quantifiers are valid in proofs, specifications, and exhaustive data. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| TN-03 | Over-hedging | Use one uncertainty marker that matches the evidence. Remove hedges that do not change the claim. | “This may perhaps offer benefits in certain situations.” | “This may offer benefits in certain situations.” | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; cautious language is appropriate when the evidence is weak or risk is high. Source pointers: [prowlo-guide](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-guide). |
| TN-04 | Stacked hedges | Do not stack modal verbs and adverbs around one claim. Choose the best calibrated qualifier. | “The change could potentially help improve retention.” | “The change may improve retention.” | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; quoted or legally required wording is exempt. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| TN-05 | Relentless balance | Do not append an automatic counterpoint to every claim. Include supplied competing evidence when it affects the decision. | “There are advantages and disadvantages, and ultimately both approaches have merit.” | State the verified deciding difference between the approaches. If no difference is supplied, do not invent one. | Editorial heuristic; balanced treatment is required in some reporting, teaching, and policy contexts. Source pointers: [th-patterns](sources-and-limits.md#source-th-patterns). |
| TN-06 | Unqualified optimism | Do not turn setbacks into generic hope. Report the supplied outcome and evidence plainly. | “Despite the setbacks, the future remains bright and full of opportunity.” | Name the documented setback and forecast. If no forecast is supported, stop after the setback. | Editorial heuristic; positive forecasts are fine when supported. Source pointers: [russell](sources-and-limits.md#source-russell), [tropes](sources-and-limits.md#source-tropes). |
| TN-07 | Formulaic resilience | Name the supplied challenge and evidence of recovery instead of claiming that an organization thrives. | “Despite ongoing challenges, the organization continues to thrive.” | If independently supplied or verified: “[Measured challenge], but [documented recovery indicator].” Otherwise, remove “continues to thrive.” | Editorial heuristic; concise summaries may use “resilient” when a measure is supplied. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| TN-08 | Bland emotional range | Describe conflict, harm, or uncertainty at the level the evidence supports. Do not sanitize it into generic growth language. | “The dispute offered valuable lessons and opportunities for growth.” | State the independently supplied or verified effect of the dispute. If no effect is known, report only that the dispute occurred. | Editorial heuristic; maintain appropriate restraint around sensitive events. Source pointers: [russell](sources-and-limits.md#source-russell). |
| TN-09 | Impersonal exposition | Use a situated speaker or named actor when the genre calls for one. Do not add first person by quota. | “Users may encounter difficulties when adapting to unfamiliar workflows.” | If independently supplied or verified: “In [named test], [measured users] had difficulty with [specific workflow].” Otherwise, retain a properly qualified general statement. | Editorial heuristic; formal, scientific, legal, and anonymous prose may require impersonal voice. Source pointers: [linguistic](sources-and-limits.md#source-linguistic). |
| TN-10 | Register mismatch | Match diction and formality to the audience, channel, and stakes. | In a group chat: “Your contribution underscores the significance of collective engagement.” | If it reflects the speaker’s meaning: “Thanks, your contribution helped the group.” Otherwise, preserve the supplied substance and simplify only the register. | Checker flag and editorial heuristic; preserve requested formality and established organizational voice. Source pointers: [humanizer](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanizer). |
| TN-11 | No conversational contractions | Use natural contractions in casual English when they fit the speaker’s voice. Do not force contractions into formal, legal, or quoted text. | “I do not think we should proceed because it is not ready.” | “I don’t think we should proceed because it isn’t ready.” | Editorial heuristic; dialect, accessibility, formality, and exact quotations control. Source pointers: [humanize](sources-and-limits.md#source-humanize). |
| TN-12 | Excessive polish for context | Keep routine messages proportionate to the situation. Remove ceremony that delays the point. | “I appreciate your thoughtful inquiry regarding our forthcoming gathering.” | “Thanks for asking about the gathering.” Add time or place only when supplied. | Editorial heuristic; formal correspondence may call for ceremonial language. Source pointers: [prowlo-guide](sources-and-limits.md#source-prowlo-guide). |

## Technical detector signals

These entries are diagnostic cautions. They are not writing targets, reliable visual judgments, or proof of authorship. Do not manipulate prose to increase detector uncertainty or evade a classifier. Use detectors only as limited evidence within a documented review process, and protect human-written work from adverse decisions based on a score alone.

| ID | Original pattern | Strict operational rule | Original example | Better example or exact action | Evidence and exception |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| DT-01 | Low perplexity / high likelihood | Do not claim that predictable wording proves AI authorship. A perplexity result is meaningful only with the named model, tokenization, text length, language, domain, and calibration set. | “The findings highlight the importance of effective communication.” Its score requires computation. | If analysis is required, report the model, score, benchmark distribution, uncertainty, and known false-positive limits. | Technical metric; cannot be guaranteed or inferred from the sentence. Never rewrite solely to raise perplexity. Source pointers: [detectgpt](sources-and-limits.md#source-detectgpt). |
| DT-02 | Low token rank | Do not treat frequent high-ranked token choices as an authorship verdict. Document the scoring model and comparison population. | A passage repeatedly chooses highly ranked next words, even without obvious repetition. | Use token-rank evidence only in a validated research protocol and pair it with independent provenance evidence. | Technical metric; genre, fluency, language proficiency, and model choice affect the score. Source pointers: [detectgpt](sources-and-limits.md#source-detectgpt). |
| DT-03 | Low predictability variation | Do not equate uniform model predictability with machine generation. Measure it only across enough text and against matched human and generated controls. | Successive sentences receive similarly low perplexity scores. | Report sentence-level distributions and overlap between control groups rather than a binary label. | Technical metric; short, edited, formulaic, or technical human prose may also be uniform. ## Crosswalk and completeness rules

No source row has been deleted as a duplicate. Related entries remain separate because they operate at different levels. For example, RH-09 covers repeated punctuation templates, FM-02 covers repeated semicolons, and FM-03 covers colon punchlines. VX-11 covers excessive connective words, while RH-12 covers unnecessary logical transitions. TN-01 covers unsupported certainty, TN-02 covers unwarranted universal scope, TN-03 covers excessive uncertainty, and TN-04 covers redundant uncertainty markers.

The catalog contains 144 stable IDs: RH-01–RH-12, VX-01–VX-12, RT-01–RT-16, ST-01–ST-14, TN-01–TN-16, PA-01–PA-15, FM-01–FM-14, CL-01–CL-22, WP-01–WP-15, and DT-01–DT-08.
